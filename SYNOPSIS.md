# Multi-agent product naming: a structured way to choose what deserves testing

Multi-agent product naming helps a team turn a product brief into a shortlist it can question, compare, and investigate. The tool gives different AI roles responsibility for creating names, finding weaknesses, representing customer perspectives, and judging the remaining options. Its output includes the reasoning behind each finalist, individual persona reactions, research gaps, and reasons other candidates were rejected. That makes the shortlist a starting point for a naming decision, with evidence and disagreement available for inspection. The [workflow specification](BUILD-SPEC.md) describes the intended decision process.

The strongest use case is a team that knows what it is building but cannot agree on what to call it. Founders, product managers, brand consultants, and nonprofit teams launching services could use it to structure that discussion. Their benefit would come from making competing assumptions visible: which audience matters most, what a name implies, and what evidence should eliminate it. This is a proposed use, rather than a demonstrated improvement in launch performance. The [input configuration](config.example.json) makes those choices explicit.

A useful run starts with documents. Users supply product information, naming history, three priority customer profiles, and two secondary profiles. They can specify rejected names, research markets, languages, and domain endings. The tool snapshots its inputs and checks source references before generating candidates. A thin brief still produces a thin foundation; more agents cannot supply customer knowledge that the team has never collected. The practical preparation is therefore to describe the product accurately and distinguish observed customer needs from assumptions. See the [source handling](naming.py).

The Creator generates twenty initial candidates using at least five naming strategies. Each candidate has a rationale, pronunciation, customer insight, strengths, weaknesses, and provisional scores. Variety is intentional: the process asks for different associations and constructions rather than twenty minor variations on a favorite phrase. These initial scores do not determine the final ranking. They explain the Creator's thinking and give later roles something specific to challenge. Teams can inspect how a proposed name connects to the brief through the [Creator instructions](prompts/creator.md).

The Adversary then investigates candidates through public web research. It records possible company conflicts, trademark leads, domain questions, app usage, and other concerns. Findings distinguish sourced observations, subjective objections, and unverified leads. That separation matters because an awkward association and an existing business with a similar name are different reasons for hesitation. Neither should disappear inside an unexplained overall score. Research requirements and evidence checks are implemented in the [runner](naming.py).

A Referee reduces the initial pool, after which the Creator can defend, concede, withdraw, or replace candidates. Replacement names receive fresh identities and fresh screening. This creates a useful opportunity to respond to criticism without pretending the original suggestion was sound. The process preserves rejected candidates, so an eliminated name cannot quietly return under a different identifier. Recent fixes also ensure the Creator sees every reserved identity and the minimum number of candidates it must retain. The [replacement tests](tests/test_replacement_context.py) exercise those rules.

Three persona judges evaluate independently. Each receives its own profile and the same product and candidate evidence, without seeing the other judges' votes or the Creator's estimates. Their reactions may expose different expectations about warmth, trust, clarity, or usefulness. A name that attracts one audience while alienating another is information the team needs, especially when those audiences have different priorities. These are simulated interpretations of supplied profiles, not interviews or measured preferences. The [pipeline tests](tests/test_pipeline.py) check that separation.

The scoring system deliberately preserves disagreement. By default, persona resonance combines the average across all three judges with additional emphasis on the strongest two responses. Other weighted criteria include distinctiveness, memorability, credibility, clarity, emotional fit, pronunciation, searchability, and extensibility. Code calculates the totals instead of accepting model arithmetic. Risks are assessed separately, and repeated versions of the same underlying issue are grouped to limit duplicate deductions. The [scoring implementation](contracts.py) makes these judgments inspectable.

The resulting benefit is decision traceability. A team can ask why a name ranked well, which audience disliked it, whether a factual conflict affected its score, and what remains unresolved. The report includes a weakest argument for each finalist rather than presenting every survivor as an obvious winner. That can support a more disciplined internal discussion and a clearer brief for customer testing or specialist review. The [report generator](naming.py) preserves these details alongside the shortlist.

Consultants could use the tool to prepare options and expose tradeoffs before a client workshop. Small product teams could use it to organize naming work when they have limited research capacity. Nonprofit teams could adapt the inputs for programs or services whose names must make sense to several audiences. These applications still require someone to decide whether the supplied profiles represent the people who matter. The [fictional example](examples/demo/) provides input structure, not validated market segments.

Operationally, the tool runs locally with Python and a signed-in Codex command line application. Live model requests use that account, and public research uses web search. Product documents are therefore processed by the external model service even though orchestration and saved files remain on the user's computer. Local configuration, private inputs, and run outputs are excluded from normal Git tracking. Users must still inspect anything they intentionally share. The [setup instructions](README.md#try-it) explain this boundary.

Failures leave records. Accepted stages can be reused when resume conditions hold, and saved rejected responses can be revalidated after a validator correction. Recent improvements tolerate quotation line wrapping while still rejecting changed wording, clarify withdrawal eligibility, and identify duplicate candidate IDs precisely. Fifty offline tests pass using synthetic evidence and fictional candidates. They check defined rules, failure handling, and recovery behavior, without establishing live completion rates or superior names. The [verification record](VERIFICATION.md) states the limits.

The final decision belongs to people who understand the intended audience. A shortlist may contain fewer than five names, and an apparently strong candidate can still require substantial investigation. The tool's value is a documented basis for deciding what deserves further attention. Actual customer response, linguistic suitability, continuing brand use, and formal clearance require separate work before commitment. The [evidence limits](README.md#evidence-limits) remain part of that decision.
